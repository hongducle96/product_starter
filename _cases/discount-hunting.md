Customers kept saying the same thing: "It's hard to find things on sale." The obvious answer was a new filter. Here is why the team did not start there.

## What the team heard

Support tickets and app reviews repeated one complaint: finding discounted items took too long. Someone in the meeting said it right away: "Let's add a sale filter." It sounded reasonable, and it would have been easy to build.

## The questions a PM asks first

Before agreeing to the filter, the PM asked four questions:

1. **Where exactly do customers get stuck?** Is it finding the sale section, or finding their size within it?
2. **How many people have this problem?** A loud few, or most shoppers?
3. **Would a filter actually solve it?** Or would people still scroll through hundreds of items?
4. **Is the real problem search, categories or sorting?** Maybe the sale items are there, just badly ordered.

> **The shift**
>
> "Add a filter" is a solution. "Shoppers can't find <mark>sale items in their size</mark>" is a problem. Start from the problem.

## Working it out together

The PM brought Design, Engineering and the business team into the same room with what they had learned. Designers sketched a few options; engineers pointed out which were cheap to try; the business team explained which discounts mattered most to the company.

## How they checked it worked

After releasing a first version, the team watched three things:

- Do customers find sale products faster?
- Do more of them buy?
- Or do they still leave without buying?

If the answer to the last question is still "yes", the team goes back around the loop. That is not failure; [that is how Product works]({{ '/days/day-1/' | relative_url }}).

> **Try this**
>
> Think of an app you use. Write down one complaint you have about it. Now write the <mark>problem behind the complaint</mark>, without naming a feature.
